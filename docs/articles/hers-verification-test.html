HERS Verification Test: An AC Plus Heating & Air Reference Guide

Updated July 2026

HERS verification test in Victorville

The HERS Verification Test, often referred to as the HERS Test or Home Energy Rating Test, serves as a state-required on-site inspection. It verifies that a newly installed air conditioning system complies with California Title 24 energy efficiency standards through direct measurement of duct leakage, airflow, refrigerant charge, and static pressure against specific pass/fail limits. An independent certified HERS rater, unaffiliated with the installing contractor, conducts every test in person and records the results in the California Energy Commission registry prior to permit closure. A passing result is mandatory for the installation to be considered legally complete. This requirement remains in effect no matter how smoothly the equipment appears to operate.

What the HERS Verification Test is

The HERS Verification Test belongs to the category of Building Energy Compliance Inspections. It shares this group with blower-door tests and insulation inspections but applies specifically to HVAC systems in new construction and qualifying replacement projects. Title 24 Verification becomes mandatory whenever a new central air conditioning system receives a permit in California, which includes Victorville and all of San Bernardino County. Neither a permit-only installation nor contractor self-certification satisfies the rule. Only a third-party HERS rater inspection for the new air conditioning system achieves compliance.

The test generates a Verification score on a 1-to-100 scale along with an Inspection status of Pass or Fail. Both values are entered into the state registry and linked to the property’s permit record.

Measurable attributes the HERS rater tests

The HERS Verification Test requirement includes five distinct measurements, each governed by a defined acceptance range under Title 24.

  • Duct leakage rate: Duct leakage is measured in CFM25. Cubic feet per minute at 25 pascals of pressure. Total duct leakage must stay below the Title 24 threshold for the system size; excessive leakage allows conditioned air to escape before it reaches living spaces.
  • Airflow measurement: Airflow is measured in CFM per ton of cooling capacity. A properly sized and installed system delivers airflow inside the range the equipment manufacturer specifies, typically 350 to 450 CFM per ton.
  • Refrigerant charge verification: The rater confirms that target superheat and subcooling stay within ±3°F of the manufacturer’s specification. A charge outside that band lowers efficiency and reduces compressor life.
  • Static pressure: Static pressure, measured in inches of water column (in. W.c.), indicates whether the duct system creates too much resistance. High static pressure makes the blower work harder and lowers delivered airflow below the tested CFM target.
  • Duct leakage to outside: In addition to total leakage, the rater measures leakage to unconditioned space such as attics, crawlspaces, and garages. That leakage produces the largest energy loss and is reported separately as a percentage or CFM25 value.

A heat pump installation requires the same five measurements because heat pumps follow the identical Title 24 compliance pathway as conventional split systems.

How the HERS Verification Test relates to the broader HVAC system

The HERS Verification Test connects equipment performance with building envelope compliance. It verifies that the complete installed system—the air handler, the condensing unit, the duct network, and the refrigerant circuit—delivers expected performance rather than simply confirming that individual components passed factory testing. A ductless mini split system skips the duct leakage portion of the test because it contains no ducts, yet refrigerant charge and airflow verification still apply when the permit calls for HERS compliance.

The governing standard is Title 24, California’s Building Energy Code, which establishes the minimum legal level for energy performance in new and replacement HVAC installations. A passing HERS Test result shows the system reaches that minimum. An unverified HVAC system installed without a completed HERS inspection leaves the homeowner with an open permit, possible resale issues, and no field-verified proof that the system achieves the efficiency rating shown on the equipment label.

What happens when a system fails the HERS Verification Test

A failed Inspection status requires the installing contractor to fix the issue and arrange a re-inspection before the permit can close. The most frequent causes of failure are duct leakage exceeding the CFM25 limit, refrigerant charge falling outside the ±3°F superheat or subcooling target, and airflow dropping below the minimum CFM per ton. Sealing duct joints, correcting refrigerant charge, and removing static pressure restrictions represent the usual remedies. The HERS rater does not make these corrections. Responsibility for correcting a failed HERS test normally rests with the licensed air conditioning contractor who performed the installation.

An unverified HVAC system that never obtains a passing result creates continued risk: the permit stays open, the efficiency rating on the AHRI certificate remains unconfirmed in the field, and utility rebate programs that demand HERS documentation cannot be claimed. Where the HERS Verification Test requirement applies in Victorville

Title 24 Verification applies to every permitted new AC installation across California. Victorville lies within San Bernardino County, which enforces this requirement. Climate Zone 14, which includes the High Desert and Victorville, uses specific duct leakage and airflow targets that differ from those in coastal zones because of the area’s extreme summer temperatures and large daily temperature swings. Passing scores from coastal climate zones do not transfer. A system that passes HERS in a coastal climate zone may not meet the CFM and leakage limits required for Climate Zone 14, so the rater uses the zone-specific values during the inspection. Scheduling a HERS rater inspection for a permitted AC installation usually occurs in tandem with the installation work so the test can finish before permit closure and the property record can reflect a documented pass result.

AC Freezing Up: An AC Plus Heating & Air Reference Guide

Updated August 2026

AC freezing up in Victorville

Evaporator coil icing, known as AC freezing up, constitutes one form of air conditioner malfunction. Ice forms on the evaporator coil surface, blocks airflow through the system, and stops the unit from transferring heat out of the building. The condition occurs when coil surface temperature falls below 32°F and causes moisture in the air to freeze on contact with the coil fins, the refrigerant lines, and the surrounding cabinet. An untreated frozen AC unit ceases to cool the space, forces the compressor to operate against a blocked coil, and can inflict permanent damage on the refrigerant circuit.

What AC freezing up is

AC freezing up represents a measurable failure state of the refrigeration cycle. In this state the evaporator coil surface temperature falls below 0°C and ice accumulates on the coil instead of condensate draining away in normal fashion. Under standard AC operation the coil remains cold enough to absorb heat while staying warm enough for water to leave as liquid. When that balance fails, refrigerant line icing spreads from the indoor coil toward the outdoor unit and the system loses its capacity to cool. Diagnosis usually requires measurement of suction pressure and coil temperature before ice removal, a procedure a licensed air conditioning contractor performs when evaluating a frozen coil.

Measurable attributes of a frozen AC unit

AC freezing up has four attributes technicians measure to confirm the diagnosis and locate the root cause.

  • Evaporator coil surface temperature: Evaporator coil surface temperature drops below 32°F during a freeze event. Normal operation keeps the coil surface between 35°F and 45°F so condensate runs off as liquid.
  • Refrigerant suction pressure: Refrigerant suction pressure falls below 60 PSI when the system is undercharged or has a metering-device fault. Low suction pressure causes the refrigerant to expand too aggressively, pulling coil temperature below the freezing point.
  • Airflow restriction: Airflow through the coil drops below the manufacturer's rated CFM when a clogged filter, closed register or blocked return cuts the volume of warm air reaching the coil. Without enough heat load, the coil temperature collapses.
  • Ice accumulation on coils: Ice accumulation on coils builds in layers, starting at the coil face and spreading to the drain pan and refrigerant lines. Thick ice is insulation, compounding the temperature drop and extending recovery time after the unit is shut off.

How AC freezing up relates to the broader cooling system

AC freezing up appears as a downstream symptom of faults that begin in other parts of the cooling system. The evaporator coil, the air filter, the blower motor, the metering device and the refrigerant charge interact to maintain coil temperature inside the correct range. A failure in any one of those components shifts the thermal balance and produces the conditions for evaporator coil icing. Technicians who service a central air conditioning system trace the freeze back through each of these components to identify the originating fault. Ductless systems follow identical physics: a ductless mini split system can develop a frozen indoor coil for the same airflow and refrigerant reasons as a ducted unit, although the ice appears on the wall-mounted air handler instead of inside a cabinet. Heat pumps remain susceptible: a heat pump running in cooling mode uses the same refrigeration cycle and freezes under identical low-pressure or low-airflow conditions. Common signs of AC freezing up and not cooling

AC freezing up and not cooling ranks as the most reported symptom, yet several other signs appear before the unit stops producing cold air completely.

  • Warm air from supply vents: Warm air from supply vents signals that ice has blocked enough coil surface to stop heat transfer, even though the blower continues running.
  • Ice on refrigerant lines: Ice on the copper refrigerant lines running from the indoor unit to the outdoor condenser confirms that refrigerant line icing has extended beyond the coil itself.
  • Water around the air handler: Water around the air handler appears when ice melts faster than the drain pan can carry it away, often after the thermostat cycles the system off.
  • Higher-than-normal indoor humidity: Higher-than-normal indoor humidity develops because a frozen coil cannot dehumidify the air passing over it.
  • Unusual hissing or bubbling sounds: Unusual hissing or bubbling sounds near the indoor unit point to refrigerant moving through a partially blocked metering device, a common trigger for low suction pressure and coil icing.

Why is my AC freezing up: root causes

The two primary causes of AC freezing remain restricted airflow and low refrigerant charge. Each produces the identical result through a separate mechanism. A dirty air filter reduces the volume of warm air crossing the coil, so the coil absorbs less heat and its surface temperature falls below 32°F. Refrigerant level matters. A refrigerant leak or an undercharge lowers suction pressure below 60 PSI, causing the remaining refrigerant to expand too far and freeze the coil from the inside out. Secondary causes include a failing blower motor that cannot move rated CFM, a stuck or undersized expansion valve, and blocked evaporator coil fins that result from years of dust accumulation.

How to fix AC freezing up

Fixing AC freezing up begins with a complete thaw before any diagnostic work starts, because measuring refrigerant pressure or airflow through an ice-blocked coil yields inaccurate readings. The steps appear below.

  • Shut the system off: Shut the system off at the thermostat and switch the fan to "on" to push room-temperature air across the coil and speed the thaw. Full thaw takes two to four hours depending on ice thickness.
  • Replace or clean the air filter: Replace or clean the air filter once the ice is gone. A filter clogged past its rated MERV capacity is the single most common cause of evaporator coil icing and the easiest fix.
  • Check all supply and return registers: Check all supply and return registers to confirm none are closed or blocked by furniture, because even two or three closed registers can drop system airflow below the manufacturer's minimum CFM.
  • Measure suction pressure: Measure suction pressure at the service port with a refrigerant gauge set. A reading below 60 PSI on a standard R-410A system typically indicates a refrigerant issue, which generally calls for a licensed professional to locate the leak and recharge the circuit.
  • Inspect the blower motor and coil fins: Inspect the blower motor and coil fins for dust buildup, bent fins or a motor running below rated speed, all of which reduce airflow independently of the filter.

Evaporator coil icing that returns after a filter change indicates a refrigerant or mechanical issue that requires pressure testing and component inspection to resolve on a permanent basis.

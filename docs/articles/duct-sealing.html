Duct sealing in Victorville

HVAC Duct Sealing serves as an HVAC System Efficiency Service that closes gaps, cracks, and disconnected joints throughout a home’s ductwork. The work prevents conditioned air from escaping before it reaches occupied areas. Technicians address supply ducts, return ducts, and the plenums connecting them to the air handler. They apply mastic sealant, metallic foil tape, or aerosol-based sealant injection to achieve full-system airtightness. In an average home, unsealed ducts lose 10 to 30 percent of conditioned air into unconditioned spaces such as attics and crawlspaces. As a result the HVAC system runs longer, works harder, and still produces uneven room temperatures. Sealing the ducts improves HVAC system performance and air conditioning efficiency, delivering one of the highest-return efficiency upgrades available to homeowners dealing with leaking ductwork.

What duct sealing is and how it works

Duct sealing consists of locating and physically closing every air leakage point so conditioned air moves only to its intended destinations. A technician begins with a pressure test that records airflow in cubic feet per minute at 25 pascals of pressure (CFM25). After the sealing work is finished the system undergoes the identical test again. The difference between the two CFM25 values supplies concrete proof of improvement. Summer attic temperatures in Victorville regularly surpass 140°F. These conditions speed duct joint failure and increase the value of duct sealing verified by CFM25 pressure testing for local homes. Three categories of sealant material address different duct systems and access limitations. Mastic sealant is a water-based material applied by brush to joints and seams; once cured it stays flexible and adheres to sheet metal, fiberglass duct board, and flexible duct. Metallic foil tape adds reinforcement to mastic on rigid duct connections and carries a high-temperature rating. Aerosol-based sealant injection, sometimes referred to as Aeroseal, pressurizes the entire duct network and releases sealant particles from within to seal leaks unreachable from the outside. Each option fits a specific duct configuration, and technicians select the appropriate method after reviewing the initial pressure test.

Measurable attributes of duct sealing

Duct sealing yields results that can be measured before and after the work rather than estimated. The following attributes describe the delivered service.

  • Air leakage reduction potential: Air leakage reduction potential ranges from 5 to 30 percent of total conditioned air, depending on the age of the duct system and how many joints were originally left unsealed.
  • Leakage rate (CFM25): Leakage rate is measured in cubic feet per minute at 25 pascals; a well-sealed residential system targets a post-service CFM25 reading that meets or falls below local energy code thresholds.
  • Airtightness (Pa): Airtightness is expressed in pascals and confirms how much pressure the sealed duct system holds; a higher post-seal pressure reading means fewer remaining leaks.
  • Sealant material type: Sealant material type determines durability and temperature rating; mastic and metallic foil tape are the standard pairing for rigid duct, while aerosol injection handles inaccessible flexible duct runs.
  • Duct system type: Duct system type (rigid sheet metal, fiberglass duct board or flexible duct) governs which sealant materials and application methods are appropriate for the job.
  • Pressure test rating: Pressure test rating records the CFM25 value pre- and post-service, giving the homeowner a documented before-and-after comparison of leakage reduction.

A central air conditioning system depends on these numbers being low. When the leakage rate stays high, the air handler runs at full capacity but the living space never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

How duct sealing relates to the broader HVAC system

Ductwork Sealing connects directly to the air distribution network and every component attached to it. A sealed duct system lowers the static pressure the air handler must overcome, which decreases motor amperage draw and lengthens blower service life. The procedure shields the furnace heat exchanger and the evaporator coil inside a heat pump from strain caused by pressure imbalances and restricted airflow. Duct sealing and duct insulation frequently appear together because sealing eliminates air loss while insulation reduces heat transfer through the duct walls; each measure corrects a separate failure mode in the same assembly.

A ductless mini split system avoids the issue by supplying conditioned air straight to each zone without any duct runs. This difference makes duct sealing especially pertinent to homes that already operate forced-air systems where the existing duct network remains and leakage constitutes the main efficiency loss. During a retrofit, airflow and duct-leakage assessments weigh sealing the current ducts against switching to a ductless system. The choice that yields the better long-term result hinges on the home layout, with an air conditioning contractor typically guiding that evaluation.

Signs of duct leakage and common problem areas

Duct leakage appears through a consistent set of symptoms rather than one dramatic failure. Rooms that stay warmer or cooler than the thermostat setting, utility bills that exceed expected usage, and visible dust buildup around supply registers rank among the clearest signals. Flexible duct connections at the air handler and at each register boot rank as frequent failure locations. The clamps and tape applied during initial installation lose integrity over time, particularly in attics subject to extreme thermal cycling.

Unsealed ductwork draws unconditioned air into the return side. Return-side leaks pull attic air, crawlspace air, and garage fumes into the airstream, which lowers indoor air quality and requires the equipment to condition air never intended for the system. Supply-side losses reduce room pressure, causing doors to slam and drawing outdoor air through building gaps. Both issues resolve once the duct system receives proper sealing followed by pressure testing that verifies the correction.

Where duct sealing applies in Victorville homes

Air Duct Sealing applies to any residence equipped with a forced-air distribution system. The requirement stands out in the Victorville area due to High Desert climate conditions. Attic temperatures that climb past 130°F during July and August hasten the breakdown of duct tape adhesives and flexible duct jackets, generating fresh leaks in systems that performed adequately only a few years earlier. Single-story homes with attic duct runs, two-story homes with duct chases inside interior walls, and older tract homes constructed before current airtightness standards represent the most frequent candidates for professional duct sealing service. Air duct sealing normally includes pressure testing plus sealing work on both rigid and flexible duct systems, bringing leakage rates down to present energy code levels in Victorville homes.
